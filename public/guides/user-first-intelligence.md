# User-First Intelligence

Understand **people** before **algorithms**: who they are, where they ask, what they need, and what to **build** for them

> User-First Intelligence (UFI) starts with people, not keywords. It follows the people you serve to every place they ask for help (search engines, AI assistants, video, social platforms, communities and reviews), sorts what they say onto one map of your industry, and turns the gaps into decisions about what to build, publish or fix, in the format and on the platform each audience prefers.

## What is User-First Intelligence?

> **In plain words:** Start with the people, not the keywords. Find out who they are, where they ask for help and what they need, put all of it on one map of your industry, and build what that map says is missing.

**User-First Intelligence (UFI)** is a framework for understanding demand from the person outward. It starts with **who people are**, follows them to **the platforms where they spend time and ask for help**, collects what they need in their own words, and places all of it on one map of an industry, so a business can decide what to build, publish or fix for each audience, in the format and on the platform that audience prefers.

> **The definition, in one sentence:** User-First Intelligence is the practice of modeling demand from people outward (who they are, where they ask, what they need) and mapping it to an industry, so that what a business builds, publishes and fixes follows its users rather than an algorithm. (Venkata Pagadala, 2026)

**The User-First Intelligence framework: five steps, one map, one rule and a loop.**

1. **Users**: Who are the people behind the questions? Start from people, not keywords.
2. **Personas**: Which groups do they form, and what do they need? Every trait graded: measured, derived or inferred.
3. **Platforms**: Where do they spend time, ask and trust? Twenty demand sources and an attention map.
4. **Content**: What answers them, and what is missing? Gaps by need, surface and format.
5. **Business**: What should we build, publish or fix? Decisions, measured as outcomes.

- **The map:** One industry ontology and knowledge graph (Entities, Needs and intents, Personas, Platforms and surfaces, Competitors, Sources, roles and dates). Every step reads and writes it.
- **The rule:** AI agents gather and classify. People decide.
- **The loop:** Measure the outcomes, watch where people move, refresh the map.

The name states the order of work. **User first**: begin with the person, not the keyword, the ranking or the model. **Intelligence**: turn scattered signals into decisions, with the evidence attached. The algorithm still matters. **It comes second**, because algorithms change every few months and the needs behind them change far more slowly.

> "Start with the person. The algorithm comes second." (Venkata Pagadala, creator of User-First Intelligence)

## How is User-First Intelligence different from SEO and keyword research?

> **In plain words:** Keyword research looks at the search box. User-First Intelligence looks at the person, and the search box is only one of the places that person asks.

**Keyword-first**

- Starts from the search box
- Counts phrases that have search volume
- Plans one platform at a time
- Keeps score with rankings
- Treats personas as slides

**User-first**

- Starts from the person and the need
- Counts every place people ask, in their own words
- Puts every platform on one industry map
- Keeps score with outcomes: tasks done, sales, customers kept
- Grades every persona trait by its evidence

A keyword tool sees only the part of demand that reaches a search box in a countable phrasing. People also ask in Facebook groups and on Reddit, watch demonstrations on YouTube and TikTok, read reviews before they buy, and increasingly put the whole question to an AI assistant. **Search is one side of the coin.**

- **Above the waterline, Search queries with volume**: What keyword tools count: one phrasing, one market, one month.
- **Long-tail and zero-volume questions**: Asked, but too rare, too new or too specific for a tool to count.
- **Conversations in communities**: Facebook groups, Reddit and forums: the context, the trade-offs, what failed.
- **Video and the comments under it**: YouTube, TikTok and Instagram: demonstrations and the questions they raise.
- **Reviews and support tickets**: What happened after the purchase, and what broke.
- **AI assistant conversations**: Private; visible only through what the engines retrieve and cite.
- **Market signals**: Earnings calls, job postings and regulations: why a need matters to the business.

*The demand iceberg: an illustration of the idea, not drawn to scale.*

| Approach | Starts from | Main data | Unit | Output | Best for | Blind spot |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Keyword research | The search box | Keyword volumes, rankings, SERP features | Keyword | A keyword list and a content calendar | Sizing search demand; SEO | Needs that never reach a search box in a countable phrasing; estimates differ by tool |
| Audience research | An audience's online footprint | Follows, mentions, sites and channels used | Audience segment | Where an audience pays attention | Media planning and outreach | What the audience needs and asks, placed on a map of the industry |
| Classic personas | Workshops and interviews | Qualitative research | Archetype | Persona documents | Shared empathy inside a team | Invented detail stated as fact; rarely updated |
| Jobs to be done | The job a customer hires a product to do | Customer interviews | Job | Job statements and desired outcomes | Product strategy | Scale, and where people express the job across platforms |
| Search everywhere optimization | Search on every platform | Queries and visibility on Google, YouTube, TikTok, Amazon and others | Platform and query | A visibility plan per platform | Distribution across platforms | Platform reports stay in silos without one industry map that decides which needs matter |
| User-First Intelligence | People, then the platforms they use | Twenty demand sources in five groups, plus the answer surfaces | A need on the industry map, for a persona, on a platform | What to build, publish or fix, in which format and where, with the evidence attached | Strategy across search, AI answers, social, product and service | Needs a designed map and disciplined evidence handling; slower to start than a keyword list |

**UFI does not replace SEO.** Search engines remain the largest demand source most businesses can measure, and technical SEO still decides whether a page can be found at all. UFI decides which needs deserve a page, a video, a product feature or nothing, and for whom; SEO, [answer engine optimization](https://venkatapagadala.com/solutions/aeo) and [generative engine optimization](https://venkatapagadala.com/guides/topics/geo) make the chosen answers easy to find.

## Why does User-First Intelligence matter now?

> **In plain words:** People ask in more places than ever, AI answers now sit between the question and the page, keyword volume misses much of what people need, and audiences keep moving between platforms.

- **18%** Google searches with an AI summary: 900 U.S. adults' browsing, March 2025
- **8% vs 15%** clicked a result, with and without a summary
- **1%** clicked a link inside the summary
- **~40%** of young U.S. users looking for lunch went to TikTok or Instagram: Google, citing its own research, 2022

*Sources: [Pew Research Center, 2025](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2025/07/22/google-users-are-less-likely-to-click-on-links-when-an-ai-summary-appears-in-the-results/); [TechCrunch, 2022](https://techcrunch.com/2022/07/12/google-exec-suggests-instagram-and-tiktok-are-eating-into-googles-core-products-search-and-maps/), where Google said the figure came from a survey of U.S. users aged 18 to 24.*

**When an AI summary appears, people click less**

| | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Clicked a result, no summary on the page | 15% of visits |
| Clicked a result, summary on the page | 8% of visits |
| Clicked a link inside the summary | 1% of visits |

*Pew Research Center, July 2025: 900 U.S. adults' Google searches in March 2025.*

1. **People ask in more places.** A homeowner with a leaking roof might ask an AI assistant, read an AI summary on Google, watch a contractor on YouTube and post in a local Reddit thread before calling anyone. Every new platform that takes off sends a keyword list out of date.
2. **AI answers sit between the question and the page.** **When an AI summary appeared, people clicked a traditional result in 8% of visits, against 15% without one.**
3. **Volume is a model, not a census.** A search volume is a tool's estimate for one phrasing, one market and one period. When I was choosing a $60,000 three-row SUV for my young family, the answers I trusted came from a Facebook group of owners: which trim, which dealer, what they wished they had known. Questions like those rarely register as volume, yet AI engines answer them by reading exactly those communities.
4. **Audiences move.** **Among U.S. teens, Facebook use fell from 71% in 2014-15 to 32% in 2024** ([Pew Research Center, 2024](https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2024/12/12/teens-social-media-and-technology-2024/)). A plan built on last year's platform map is a plan for last year's audience.

> "Zero volume does not mean zero demand." (Venkata Pagadala, creator of User-First Intelligence)

> **What User-First Intelligence is not:** It is not a tool, though tools can run it. It is not a replacement for SEO. It is not surveillance: it works from public, licensed and consented data, in aggregate, and private conversations are never a listening feed. And it is not demographic destiny: age, place and identity are starting points to test, never a verdict on what a person wants.

## What are the principles of User-First Intelligence?

> **In plain words:** Eight rules hold the framework together. Every study can be checked against them.

- **01: People before keywords**: Start with who is asking and why. A keyword is one way one person phrases a need, on one platform, at one moment.
- **02: Search is one source of many**: Search engines show one side of the coin. Communities, video, reviews, marketplaces, AI assistants and the market itself show the rest.
- **03: Zero volume is not zero demand**: A volume estimate describes a tool's view of one phrasing. **When it reads zero, ask where else the need is expressed.**
- **04: Keep evidence in its lane**: A competitor's page is evidence of coverage, not of demand. **Label every claim measured, derived or inferred**, and keep its source and date.
- **05: Design the map before you fill it**: An ontology drawn from broad category to specific need, against the questions it must answer, turns scattered signals into decisions.
- **06: Format follows platform**: And platform follows persona. Text is not the answer everywhere; choose the form from the person, the place and the task.
- **07: People move; watch the movement**: Audiences migrate and rivals arrive from below and from next door. **Track the series, not the snapshot.**
- **08: Agents gather; people decide**: AI agents collect and classify at scale. People set the questions, check the samples and make the calls.

## How does the User-First Intelligence framework work?

> **In plain words:** Five questions, answered in order, all writing to one shared map of the industry. Machines do the gathering and sorting; people make the decisions.

- **Step 1 · Users: Who are the people behind the questions?**: Write down their situations, the tasks they are trying to finish and what stands in their way. "Switching providers" can mean a person worried about losing a number, a family comparing total cost or a business moving forty lines. Output: situations and tasks
- **Step 2 · Personas: Which groups do they form?**: Group people into personas and grade every trait: **measured, derived or inferred**. Age, life stage, interests, constraints and channel habits all belong; none decides behavior on its own. Output: evidence-graded personas
- **Step 3 · Platforms: Where do they spend time, ask and trust?**: Map the three relations for each persona and platform, then **collect what people say there, in their own words, with the source and date of every record**. Output: an attention map and a demand log
- **Step 4 · Content: What answers them, and what is missing?**: Place every record on the industry map, then compare demand with your coverage, your competitors' and the best available answer, surface by surface. Output: ranked gaps, each with its format
- **Step 5 · Business: What should we build, publish or fix?**: Rank the gaps by value and effort and decide. **Some of the best findings are not content at all**: reviews about long waits point to a booking feature first. Output: decisions and their measured results
- **The rule: Agents gather and classify; people decide**: Agents collect, classify and watch for change; people write the questions, design the map and check samples. See the [guide to AI agents for marketing](https://venkatapagadala.com/guides/ai-agents-for-marketing). Every decision keeps its evidence

### Technical deep dive: the record every signal becomes

Every question, post, comment and review becomes one record with its provenance attached. Roles come from a closed list (structure, coverage, answerability, demand); entities and intents come from the ontology; personas and journey stages come from the persona framework. Nothing is stored that the decision does not need.

*A demand record (JSON)*

```
{
  "id": "rec_000184",
  "text": "Which trim fits two car seats and adults in the third row?",
  "surface": "community_thread",
  "source_url": "https://example.com/thread/0184",
  "collected_at": "2026-10-23",
  "role": "demand",
  "language": "en",
  "entity": "three_row_suv.seating",
  "intent": "compare",
  "journey_stage": "consider",
  "persona": "parent_young_children",
  "label_confidence": 0.86
}
```

Two fields carry most of the weight. **role** stops coverage from posing as demand. **collected_at** makes every count a dated observation, so a later run is compared with an earlier one rather than mixed into it.

## Where do people ask? The twenty demand sources

> **In plain words:** Search is one source. Here are twenty, in five groups, each answering a different question about the same need.

- **Group 1: Search and language**: What people type, and what the words mean: the entities and terms around a topic. (Search demand, Wikis and reference) Watch out: volume is an estimate for one phrasing.
- **Group 2: Social conversations**: Questions in context: what people tried, which answer failed, which trade-off they cannot resolve. (YouTube, X, Reddit, TikTok, Facebook groups, Instagram) Watch out: one lively thread does not size a market.
- **Group 3: Customer experience**: What customers valued, what disappointed them and how they describe the gap. (Google Business Profile reviews, Marketplaces, App reviews) Watch out: reviews show a reason to investigate, not the cause.
- **Group 4: Market context**: Pricing, priorities, investment and constraints: why a need matters to the business. (Competitors, Public data, Earnings calls, Job postings, Regulations) Watch out: coverage is not demand.
- **Group 5: Expert knowledge**: Specialist interpretation and the questions just starting to appear. (Conferences, Newsletters, Podcasts, Research papers) Watch out: experts lead; check whether customers follow.
- **Handle with care: AI assistants and private messages**: AI conversations are private: observe what engines retrieve and cite, and treat any "AI search volume" as a vendor's model. Snapchat, WhatsApp and direct messages are not a listening feed; learn their role from interviews and surveys.

I presented the twenty-source map at [brightonSEO San Diego 2026](https://venkatapagadala.com/research-and-talks/brightonseo-san-diego-2026) and wrote it up for [Previsible](https://previsible.com/brightonseo/demand-research-beyond-keywords/). **Follow the need across sources**: if it appears in queries, community threads, video comments and reviews, those views should inform one decision, not four departmental reports.

### Technical deep dive: collecting responsibly

- **Public, licensed or consented only.** Respect each platform's terms and robots rules; never scrape private groups or messages.
- **Aggregate before you publish.** Report themes and counts, not people; strip names and handles from anything that leaves the analysis.
- **Archive the raw response, then parse.** A paid or rate-limited source cannot always be re-read, and fields discarded at parse time are gone.
- **Date-stamp every record** and keep the collection method beside it, so two runs can be compared like with like.
- **Count what came back empty.** A surface that produced nothing is a finding about coverage, not an absence of demand.

- **Visual and quick: How it looks, how to set it up**: A short vertical video where the persona already watches, linked to a page with the full steps.
- **Complex decision: Plans, prices, trade-offs**: A comparison page with a clear table and honest trade-offs, written to be quoted by AI answers and found in search.

- **Local and now: Open now, wait times, book a visit**: Accurate local listings, a working booking flow and a page about what to expect. Content alone will not fix a broken visit.
- **Professional buyer: Buying for a team or a committee**: Long-form guides, data and talks on LinkedIn, in newsletters and at conferences, where professionals build trust.
- **An agent buys: Compare, book or buy on the person's behalf**: Structured, machine-readable offers (schema.org markup, feeds, clear policies and prices) so an agent can verify every fact it needs.

### Ontology: design the classes before the data arrives

An ontology defines what kinds of things exist in an industry and how they relate: products, problems, needs, places, people, providers. Design it top down, from broad category to specific need, with modifiers that cut across every branch. **Write the competency questions first**, the questions the map must answer, such as "which storm-damage needs, in which towns, have no good answer?" ([Ren et al., 2014](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-319-07443-6_50)). See the [ontology topic](https://venkatapagadala.com/guides/topics/ontology).

1. **Roofing**: Industry
2. **Residential**: Segment
3. **Repair**: Service
4. **Storm damage**: Problem
5. **Hail damage**: Specific need
6. **Insurance claim**: Task

*One path through a roofing ontology, from broad category to the task a person is trying to finish. Modifiers cut across every level: location, price, urgency, comparison.*

### Industry ontology

An industry ontology is a designed map of an industry: the kinds of things in it, the relations between them and the specific things in each kind, from broad category to specific need.

UFI designs the map before it collects the data, top down from category to need, with modifiers that cut across every branch: location, price, urgency, comparison. The competency questions, the questions the map must be able to answer, are written first, so the map serves the decisions rather than whatever data arrived first.

- **Analogy:** The shelving plan of a library, drawn before the books arrive.
- **Example:** Roofing, then residential, then repair, then storm damage, then hail damage, then an insurance claim, with location and urgency as modifiers.
- **In the framework:** The one map, its schema.

### Knowledge graph: connect the entities to everything around them

A knowledge graph holds the specific entities (products, brands, places, conditions, competitors) and the links between them. Google brought the idea to search in 2012 as "things, not strings" ([Google, 2012](https://blog.google/products/search/introducing-knowledge-graph-things-not/)). In UFI the graph reaches past the company: **an industrial context graph covers the industry and its adjacent markets**, so a rival arriving from next door appears on the same map. See the [knowledge graphs topic](https://venkatapagadala.com/guides/topics/knowledge-graphs) and the [graph types guide](https://venkatapagadala.com/guides/graph-types-for-ai-agents).

### Industrial context graph (aka industry knowledge graph)

An industrial context graph is a knowledge graph of an industry and its adjacent markets that links entities to the personas, problems, platforms, places, products and competitors around them, with the source of every link.

Covering the adjacent markets means a rival arriving from next door appears on the same map as the incumbents. Every statement carries its source, its role and the date it was retrieved, so a conclusion can always be traced back to the evidence that supports it.

- **Analogy:** An atlas that shows the neighboring countries, not only your own.
- **Example:** Home internet linked to wireless carriers, satellite providers and municipal fiber, because all of them answer the same household's need.
- **In the framework:** The one map, its contents.

### Source roles: keep evidence in its lane

Each source gets exactly one role. **Structure** says what exists. **Coverage** says who answers. **Answerability** says whether the best answer is good enough. **Demand** says who asks. **Coverage never counts as demand**: that one rule prevents the most common error in content research, copying what competitors publish and calling it what people want.

### Source role

A source role says what a piece of evidence can prove: structure (what exists), coverage (who answers), answerability (whether the best answer is good enough) or demand (who asks).

Each source has exactly one role, and conclusions are drawn only from the role that can support them. The rule that coverage never counts as demand prevents the most common error in content research: copying what competitors publish and calling it what people want.

- **Analogy:** Witnesses who may testify only about what they saw.
- **Example:** A competitor's three hundred location pages are coverage. Forum threads asking which provider serves a street are demand.
- **In the framework:** Keeps the map honest.

### Intent: why the person is asking

Every record is classified twice: **where** it sits on the map, and **why** the person is asking: to research, compare, purchase, get support or reach something they already know. [Broder (2002)](https://doi.org/10.1145/792550.792552) separated navigational, informational and transactional web searches; what is new is that the same words carry different intent in a search box, an AI assistant, a forum and a video comment, so UFI labels intent per surface and records [named entities](https://venkatapagadala.com/notebook/ai/encyclopedia/named-entity) as it goes.

### Technical deep dive: the ontology and a competency question in code

A fragment of an industry ontology in Turtle, then one competency question compiled to SPARQL. Both are sketches of the pattern, not a production schema.

*Ontology fragment (Turtle)*

```
@prefix ufi:  <https://venkatapagadala.com/ufi#> .
@prefix owl:  <http://www.w3.org/2002/07/owl#> .
@prefix rdfs: <http://www.w3.org/2000/01/rdf-schema#> .

ufi:Need          a owl:Class .
ufi:StormDamage   a owl:Class ; rdfs:subClassOf ufi:Need .
ufi:HailDamage    a owl:Class ; rdfs:subClassOf ufi:StormDamage .
ufi:Town          a owl:Class .
ufi:Page          a owl:Class .

ufi:about     a owl:ObjectProperty ; rdfs:range ufi:Need .
ufi:inTown    a owl:ObjectProperty ; rdfs:range ufi:Town .
ufi:answers   a owl:ObjectProperty ; rdfs:domain ufi:Page ; rdfs:range ufi:Need .
ufi:role      a owl:ObjectProperty .   # structure | coverage | answerability | demand
```

*Competency question: which hail-damage needs, in which towns, have demand and no page that answers them? (SPARQL)*

```
PREFIX ufi:  <https://venkatapagadala.com/ufi#>
PREFIX rdfs: <http://www.w3.org/2000/01/rdf-schema#>

SELECT ?town ?need (COUNT(?ask) AS ?asks)
WHERE {
  ?ask  ufi:role ufi:Demand ; ufi:about ?need ; ufi:inTown ?town .
  ?need rdfs:subClassOf* ufi:HailDamage .
  FILTER NOT EXISTS {
    ?page ufi:role ufi:Coverage ; ufi:answers ?need ; ufi:inTown ?town .
  }
}
GROUP BY ?town ?need
ORDER BY DESC(?asks)
```

Two rates tell you whether the map works: the share of competency questions the ontology can express at all (schema pass rate), and the share the graph can actually answer (instance coverage). The open [ufi package](https://github.com/VenkataPagadalaGIT/ufi) reports both.

## Where does retrieval fit? From embeddings to AI answers

> **In plain words:** Retrieval means finding the closest matches fast. UFI uses it to sort millions of questions onto the map, and AI engines use it to decide which pages to read and cite.

### Inside: retrieval sorts demand onto the map

1. **Collect**: Records with source, role and date
2. **Embed**: Each record becomes a vector
3. **Retrieve**: Nearest nodes on the map
4. **Decide**: A model picks one, or none
5. **Check**: Gold sample and review queue
6. **Map**: Entity, intent, persona, stage

*Classification at scale, in two passes: retrieval narrows each record to a few candidates, then a model makes the call.*

[Embeddings](https://venkatapagadala.com/notebook/ai/encyclopedia/embeddings) turn each question, review and post into a vector, and a [vector index](https://venkatapagadala.com/notebook/ai/encyclopedia/vector-databases) finds the nearest nodes of the ontology in milliseconds. The expensive step, a model's decision, runs only on a handful of candidates, with the graph supplying each node's definition, neighbors and examples. **This is retrieval-augmented generation turned inward** ([Lewis et al., 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.11401)): the pattern that grounds a chatbot in documents grounds a classifier in an industry map, and graph-based retrieval extends it to questions about the whole corpus ([Edge et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.16130)). **Sort into a map you designed rather than clustering**: clusters describe the data you happened to collect, not the industry.

### Outside: AI engines retrieve before they answer

**Which three-row SUV is best for a family with two car seats?** (One question from one parent) fans out into:

- three-row SUV third-row legroom for adults
- car seat fit, three-row SUV, LATCH positions
- hybrid three-row SUV real-world fuel economy
- three-row SUV owner reviews, common problems
- best time of year to buy a three-row SUV
- three-row SUV insurance cost for a young family

*Query fan-out, illustrated: the kind of sub-searches an engine issues for one question. Examples, not a capture of any engine's real queries.*

Google describes its AI Mode as using a **query fan-out** technique: it breaks a question into subtopics and issues many searches at once on the person's behalf ([Google, 2025](https://blog.google/products/search/google-search-ai-mode-update/)). **A brand is chosen by the sub-questions, not only the head question**, and each engine reads different kinds of sources, so **map how each need fans out** (try the [Fan-out Journey](https://venkatapagadala.com/personas/fanout-journey)) and measure presence answer surface by answer surface.

### Answer surface

An answer surface is where a person receives an answer: a results page, an AI Overview, a chat assistant, a video, a marketplace listing or a store page.

AI answer engines retrieve and cite different kinds of sources, so the same need can be won on one surface and lost on another. UFI measures presence surface by surface instead of assuming one ranking stands for all.

- **Analogy:** The different counters where a customer can be served.
- **Example:** A brand cited in Google's AI Overviews for a need can be absent from a chat assistant's answer to the same question.
- **In the framework:** Steps 4 and 5.

### Technical deep dive: two-pass classification in code

*Pass 1 retrieves, pass 2 decides, a gold sample checks (Python-style sketch)*

```
# Pass 1: retrieval narrows each record to a few candidate nodes
q = embed(record.text)                        # one vector per record
candidates = index.search(q, k=8)             # cosine similarity over node vectors

# Pass 2: a model decides, with the graph's context and a fixed output schema
context = [graph.describe(node) for node in candidates]   # definition, neighbors, examples
label = llm.classify(
    text=record.text,
    candidates=context,
    schema={
        "node": "one of the candidates, or NONE",
        "intent": ["research", "compare", "purchase", "support", "navigate"],
        "confidence": "0 to 1",
        "reason": "one sentence",
    },
)

# Quality: agreement with a gold sample labeled by people
kappa = cohen_kappa(gold.labels, model.labels)   # report with n and an interval
```

Cosine similarity, cos(q, n) = q·n / (‖q‖ ‖n‖), ranks the candidates. Allowing **NONE** stops forced labels; rejected records go to a review queue, and recurring rejects show where the map needs a new node. Cost stays flat as the ontology grows, because the model only ever sees the few candidates retrieval hands it.

## How do you find the gaps that matter?

> **In plain words:** A gap is something people clearly need that nobody answers well where they look. Find them by comparing demand with what you, your competitors and the best answers already cover.

Axes: How much people need it (left to right) and How well it is answered (bottom to top).

- **Maintain or prune** (High how well it is answered, low how much people need it): Well answered, little need. Keep it accurate; do not invest more.
- **Defend** (High on both): Big need, well answered. Keep it fresh and watch the competitors.
- **Watch** (Low on both): Little need, little coverage. Early signals can move it right.
- **Close the gap** (Low how well it is answered, high how much people need it): Big need, poorly answered. This is where to build, publish or fix first.

*The gap matrix: every node on the map lands in one quadrant, per answer surface.*

Gaps are an outcome of **query discovery**, the framework's first task: **find the questions people ask about the industry's entities, on any surface and in their own words, before deciding which to answer**. A **Content Gap Score** turns the comparison into a ranking; its open reference implementation is published on [GitHub](https://github.com/VenkataPagadalaGIT/ufi).

### Technical deep dive: the Content Gap Score

The score ranks entities on the map. Every term comes from one source role, so coverage can never pose as demand, and reported search volume is not an input at all.

*From the open ufi package's README*

```
prior(e) = β·rel(e) + (1 − β)·dem(e)
CGS(e)   = prior(e) · (1 − own(e)) · [ α·(1 − cov_K(e)) + (1 − α)·mean_q(1 − ans(q,e)) ]

rel(e)    topic-sensitive PageRank over the structure graph, rank-normalized    (structure)
dem(e)    rank-normalized count of user questions linked to e                   (demand)
own(e)    the organization's own coverage, graded 0, ½ or 1                     (coverage)
cov_K(e)  mean of the same grade over the competitor set K                      (coverage)
ans(q,e)  whether the web answers competency question q about e                 (answerability)
```

α moves weight between the two deficits; β between structure and demand. The first bracket term is the instance gap (nobody covers this entity), the second the schema gap (nobody answers this question about it); they are reported separately because they call for different content. The score is a specification: no published study has yet tested what it predicts.

Audience migration is a lasting shift in where a group spends time, asks questions or trusts answers, from one platform or channel to another.

## What happens when every result is personal?

> **In plain words:** Your results are not my results, and AI answers change even between two tries. So stop chasing one position, and know your people well enough to be present wherever their answers come from.

Search results already vary from person to person, and AI answers vary more. **A 2013 audit of Google found that personalization changed about 11.7% of results on average**, driven mainly by being signed in and by location ([Hannak et al., 2013](https://doi.org/10.1145/2488388.2488435)). AI assistants compose their answers instead of ranking pages: ask the same question twice and the answer, and its sources, can change, and researchers at OpenAI found that a chatbot's responses can differ with nothing more than the user's name ([Eloundou et al., 2025](https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2025/hash/92af0c8c2664429de2bb44c2692d84ae-Abstract-Conference.html)). As assistants remember more about each user, that variation will grow.

> "What your tools see is not what your users see." (Venkata Pagadala, creator of User-First Intelligence)

**What a rank tracker sees**

- One query, one phrasing
- One location, signed out
- One results page, one position
- A snapshot of a moving target

**What User-First Intelligence measures**

- Each persona's questions, in their own words
- Every engine and surface they use
- Presence and citations across repeated runs
- Outcomes: tasks finished, sales, customers kept

Here is where I think this goes. **These are predictions, not findings**:

- **Prediction 1: Position will matter less, presence more**: When every result is personal there is no single rank to win. What a brand controls is whether it is present, and trusted, in the sources each engine reads for each persona.
- **Prediction 2: Optimize for the persona, not the position**: The stable unit is not a keyword at rank three. It is a need, held by a persona, on a platform, and it survives personalization because it describes the person.
- **Prediction 3: Personal agents will standardize demand, not end it**: When people hand tasks to their own agents (a flight that lands before noon, a checked bag, under $500), the agents ask in patterns their principals set. Needs repeat across a persona. **Publish offers an agent can verify.**
- **Prediction 4: Measurement moves to panels and outcomes**: Teams will sample answers through persona panels, prompts in each persona's words run across engines, and judge success by completed tasks and revenue.

> "Agents gather. People decide." (Venkata Pagadala, creator of User-First Intelligence)

### Technical deep dive: measuring answers when results are personal

- **Build a persona panel.** For each persona and need, write prompts in that persona's own words (from the demand log, not from a keyword list), with the context they would give an assistant.
- **Repeat runs.** Run every prompt on every engine several times; a single run of a generative answer is an anecdote.
- **Measure presence with an interval.** Presence rate = runs that cite you ÷ runs; report a Wilson interval so small panels do not overclaim.
- **Measure stability.** Jaccard overlap of cited domains between runs and between engines, J(A, B) = |A ∩ B| ÷ |A ∪ B|, shows how much the answer moves.
- **Watch drift.** Re-run the same panel on a schedule; a falling presence rate with a stable panel is answer drift, not a change in demand.

## How do you run a User-First Intelligence study?

> **In plain words:** Seven steps from a business question to a decision. A small business can do it in a week with a spreadsheet; a large one runs it continuously with agents.

- **Step 1: Frame the decision and write the questions first.** Decide what the study must inform (a content plan, a product, a market entry) and write the competency questions the map must answer before collecting any data.
- **Step 2: Define the personas and grade the evidence.** Record each persona's situation, tasks, constraints and channel habits, and mark every trait measured, derived or inferred.
- **Step 3: Map platforms and attention.** For each persona, note where it spends time, where it asks and where it trusts answers. Use survey data as the prior and your own analytics as the check.
- **Step 4: Collect demand in people's own words.** Pull questions, posts, comments, reviews and queries from the platforms on the attention map, keeping each record's source, role and date. Keep competitors' coverage apart from demand.
- **Step 5: Classify everything onto one industry map.** Place each record by entity, need, intent, persona and journey stage. Let AI agents do the first pass, check a gold sample by hand and fix the map where it fails.
- **Step 6: Compare demand, supply and competitors to find the gaps.** For every node, compare the need with your coverage, your competitors' coverage and the quality of the best available answer, surface by surface, and rank the gaps by value and effort.
- **Step 7: Decide, ship, measure and refresh.** Choose what to build, publish or fix, in which format and on which platform. Measure completed tasks and business outcomes, re-run the collection on a schedule and watch where people move.

## How do you measure User-First Intelligence?

> **In plain words:** Measure what changed for people and for the business first. Rankings still count where they exist; they are no longer the scoreboard.

- **Outcomes: Tasks, sales, customers kept**: Qualified visits, leads, sales and retention, traced to the decisions the study produced.
- **Coverage: Priority nodes answered well**: The share of priority nodes you answer well, per answer surface: results, AI answers, video, communities, reviews.
- **Map quality: Questions the map can answer**: The share of competency questions the ontology can express, and the share the graph actually answers.
- **Classification: Agreement with people**: Agreement with a gold sample checked by people, reported with its sample size.
- **Evidence: Measured, not inferred**: The share of persona traits and claims that are measured, and how old the evidence is.
- **Instruments: One figure per instrument**: Volume and traffic estimates from different tools are different models. **Compare like with like and never mix them in one chart.**

## How does User-First Intelligence relate to existing ideas?

> **In plain words:** It stands on decades of well-tested ideas and combines them into one working system.

- **1986: User-centered design.** Norman and Draper put the user at the center of system design. UFI applies that stance to demand research and content.
- **1999: Personas.** Alan Cooper made users concrete for product teams. UFI grades every persona trait by its evidence.
- **2002: A taxonomy of web search.** [Broder](https://doi.org/10.1145/792550.792552) separated navigational, informational and transactional searches: intent, classified.
- **2010: People ask their networks.** [Morris, Teevan and Panovich](https://doi.org/10.1145/1753326.1753587) showed people put questions to their social networks, not only to search engines.
- **2012: Market structure from text.** [Netzer, Feldman, Goldenberg and Fresko](https://doi.org/10.1287/mksc.1120.0713) mapped a market's structure from forum text.
- **2014: Competency questions.** [Ren et al.](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-319-07443-6_50) used competency questions to decide what an ontology must answer.
- **2016: Jobs to be done; competition from search data.** Christensen, Hall, Dillon and Duncan asked what a customer hires a product to do; [Ringel and Skiera](https://doi.org/10.1287/mksc.2015.0950) mapped competition among more than a thousand products from search data.
- **2019: Customer needs from user-generated content.** [Timoshenko and Hauser](https://doi.org/10.1287/mksc.2018.1123) found customer needs in user-generated content with machine learning.
- **2024: Generative engine optimization.** [Aggarwal et al.](https://doi.org/10.1145/3637528.3671900) studied how sources gain visibility in AI-generated answers; Google's [people-first content](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/creating-helpful-content) guidance points the same way.
- **2026: User-First Intelligence.** **The combination, run as one system**: a designed industry map every demand source lands on; evidence with roles and grades; persona, platform and format decided together; agents at scale; and a loop that watches where people move.

## Frequently asked questions about User-First Intelligence

### What is User-First Intelligence?

User-First Intelligence (UFI) is a framework, created by Venkata Pagadala, for understanding demand from people outward: who they are, where they spend time and ask for help, what they need in their own words, and how that maps to an industry, so a business decides what to build, publish or fix for each audience.

### Who created User-First Intelligence?

Venkata Pagadala, a search and AI product leader and researcher. It grew out of his User-First Optimization posts in 2024 and 2025 and his brightonSEO San Diego 2026 talk, with the line "user first, algorithm second", and he named and specified it as User-First Intelligence in October 2026.

### Is User-First Intelligence the same as SEO?

No. SEO makes pages easy for search engines to find and show. UFI decides which needs matter, for whom and where, before any optimization. SEO, GEO and AEO are among its outputs, and it also produces decisions about video, communities, products and service.

### Does zero search volume mean zero demand?

No. Search volume is a tool's estimate for one phrasing, one market and one period. People often express the same need in communities, reviews, videos and AI assistants before, or instead of, typing a countable query. Zero volume is a reason to look elsewhere, not a verdict.

### What data does User-First Intelligence use?

Twenty demand sources in five groups (search and language, social conversations, customer experience, market context and expert knowledge), plus the answer surfaces where people receive answers. It works from public, licensed and consented data, in aggregate, and keeps the source, role and date of every record.

### Do I need a knowledge graph to start?

No. Start with a one-page map: your industry's main categories, the needs under each and the personas who hold them. A graph pays off when sources, markets and competitors multiply and you need to connect them.

### How does User-First Intelligence handle AI search?

AI assistants are both a demand surface and an answer surface. UFI maps the sub-questions an engine fans a question out into, measures presence and citations engine by engine, and treats vendor figures for AI prompt volume as models to check, not counts.

### How do you target an audience when search and AI results are personalized?

Target the unit that personalization does not change: a need, held by a persona, on a platform. Be present and trusted in the sources each engine reads for that persona, and measure outcomes instead of one tracked position that no customer sees.

### Will personal AI agents make demand research obsolete?

No. Agents will ask on behalf of people, in patterns their principals set, and those needs and constraints repeat across a persona. Knowing the patterns, and publishing offers an agent can verify, becomes more valuable, not less.

### Can a small business use User-First Intelligence?

Yes. Pick three personas, five sources and a one-page map, run the seven steps in a week, and act on the top three gaps. The method scales down; only the tooling grows.

### Is User-First Intelligence ethical?

It is designed to be: public, licensed and consented data only, in aggregate; private conversations are never a listening feed; personas are graded by evidence; and identity is never used to decide what a person is offered or denied.
